
# 🚀 Project Ideas and Brainstorming

This page helps participants explore ideas, study previous NASA Space Apps projects, and develop original solutions.

The ideas and examples here are starting points for learning and brainstorming. They are not official NASA challenge statements or guaranteed winning ideas.

## 🔎 Learn from Previous Space Apps Projects

One of the best ways to prepare for NASA Space Apps is to explore projects from previous years.

Do not limit your research to the Global Winners. Also explore Global Nominees, Global Finalists, and Honorable Mentions. Many creative projects deserve attention even if they did not receive a global award.

### What Should You Study?

When exploring a previous project:

1. Read its original challenge statement.
2. Understand the problem the team wanted to solve.
3. Identify the NASA datasets or other sources it used.
4. Explore its website, prototype, demonstration, or presentation where available.
5. Understand the technologies and methods used.
6. Identify what made the project useful, creative, or different.
7. Think about what could be improved or explored further.

**Don't just look at the final result. Understand the thinking behind it.**

## 💡 Where Can You Find Original Ideas?

Great ideas do not always begin with complicated technology.

Start by observing problems, asking questions, and exploring scientific data.

### Earth and Climate

- Build a dashboard that helps people explore a selected Earth observation dataset over time.
- Create an educational visualization explaining how satellites measure environmental variables.
- Explore how environmental conditions differ across locations.
- Develop a tool that helps users understand changes in forests, oceans, or land cover.

### Space Science and Exploration

- Create an interactive explainer for a planetary mission or scientific concept.
- Visualize milestones from an important space mission.
- Build a learning tool for exploring astronomical images and their metadata.
- Create an educational simulation that explains a scientific concept.

### Data Storytelling and Education

- Turn a complex dataset into a clear, accessible story.
- Create a glossary that explains scientific variables in simple language.
- Develop an interactive science quiz or educational game.
- Build a searchable catalog of datasets related to a specific question.
- Create a digital experience that helps younger students explore Earth and space science.

### Local Problems, Global Data

Think about how NASA data could help people understand problems in their own communities.

For example:

- Explore environmental changes in a particular region.
- Investigate how satellite observations can support agricultural awareness.
- Create visual explanations of floods, droughts, or changing weather patterns.
- Help students understand how Earth observation connects to everyday life.

A local problem can inspire a project with broader relevance.

## 🧠 How to Develop a Unique Idea

Finding an idea is only the beginning. The next step is to turn it into something meaningful and testable.

Try this process:

1. **Explore:** Read the official challenge statements carefully.
2. **Research:** Investigate the problem using reliable sources.
3. **Discover:** Explore existing projects and solutions.
4. **Identify gaps:** Ask what is missing, difficult, or not addressed.
5. **Brainstorm:** Generate several possible approaches.
6. **Validate:** Find real datasets that can support your idea.
7. **Prototype:** Build a small working version of your solution.
8. **Improve:** Test it, collect feedback, and refine it.

### Questions to Ask Yourself

- What specific problem does this project address?
- Who would benefit from this solution?
- Does something similar already exist?
- What could make our approach different or more useful?
- Which real dataset supports the idea?
- Can we realistically build a prototype?
- How will we test whether it works?
- What are its limitations?

**Remember:** Being unique does not necessarily mean inventing something nobody has ever imagined. It can mean solving an existing problem in a more useful, accessible, or creative way.

## ⚠️ Important Reminders

- Previous winners are sources of inspiration, not guaranteed formulas for success.
- Do not copy another team's project, design, presentation, or code.
- Always credit original research, datasets, and third-party resources.
- Prioritize the actual problem described in the current year's challenge.
- Avoid adding AI, 3D graphics, or complex technologies unless they genuinely improve the solution.
- A clear, functional prototype can be more meaningful than an ambitious idea that cannot be demonstrated.
